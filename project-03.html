Case Study · 03 / 2025

PANKH
AUR GOONJ.

Immersive VR Experience · 3D Design · Interactive UI · Unity

An immersive VR experience where users explore a forest, identify birds through their calls, and interact with nature using binoculars and guided voice UI. A multi-sensory journey combining education and play to reconnect people with biodiversity.

Role
3D Assets · UI Design · Interactive Elements
Type
Collaborative VR Project
Tools

Platform

01Project Overview

A forest you can hear before you see.

Pankh Aur Goonj (Pankh = Wings, Goonj = Echo) is a VR experience set inside a realistic Indian forest. Users spot birds through binoculars, identify them by their calls, and learn about each species through interactive voice-guided UI.

I contributed 3D asset creation, UI design, and interactive element implementation as part of a multidisciplinary team, working across design and development in Unity.

My Contributions
3D modelling of environmental assets, UI layout and interaction design for the in-VR interface, implementation of interactive elements and collider-based warning systems in Unity.
Team Context
Collaborative, multidisciplinary project. My role bridged design and development - responsible for both the visual language of the experience and the interactive logic that brought it to life.
02Problem Statement

People are disconnecting from nature.

In today's fast-paced digital world, people - especially younger generations - are increasingly disconnected from nature. Traditional educational tools about biodiversity are passive and forgettable.

There is a lack of engaging, interactive tools that foster genuine curiosity about bird species and their natural habitats - experiences that feel immersive enough to create lasting memory and awareness.

How Might We
"How might we use VR to create an educational experience about birds that is immersive, multi-sensory, and engaging enough to build lasting curiosity about nature?"
03Solution

Education through immersion and play.

Pankh Aur Goonj offers a complete multi-sensory VR environment where sound is the primary navigation tool. Users hear a bird call, locate it through binoculars, and trigger an interactive information overlay - learning by doing rather than reading.

The experience combines realistic 3D forest environments, spatial audio, and voice-assisted guidance to make biodiversity education feel like exploration rather than instruction.

01
Binocular Bird Spotting
Users physically raise binoculars in VR to scan the forest canopy. A zoom mechanic rewards patient, careful observation.
02
Bird Call Identification
Spatial audio cues guide users toward hidden birds. Identifying a call triggers the bird's information panel in the environment.
03
Voice-Assisted Guidance
A calm narrator guides users through the forest, providing contextual information without breaking immersion.
04
Interactive UI Panels
Bird info cards appear in world-space when a species is identified - species name, habitat, call type, and conservation status.
05
Realistic Forest Environment
Custom 3D assets including trees, foliage, terrain, and ambient wildlife were built to create a believable Indian forest ecosystem.
06
Warning System with Colliders
Collider-triggered alerts notify users when they approach boundaries or restricted zones, maintaining safety and flow.
07
Bird Reactive Behaviour
Birds respond to user proximity and movement - taking flight, calling, or settling. Small cues that dramatically increase realism.
04My Contributions

Bridging design and development.

My role in this project spanned three areas: creating the visual building blocks of the world, designing how users interact with information inside VR, and implementing the logic that made those interactions work.

01
3D Assets
Modelled and textured environmental assets in Blender - trees, foliage, rocks, and ground cover - optimised for real-time VR rendering without compromising visual quality.
02
UI Design
Designed the in-VR information panels, HUD elements, and interaction prompts. All UI was designed for readability at VR viewing distances with appropriate contrast and scale.
03
Interactive Elements
Implemented collider-based triggers, bird reactive behaviours, and the binocular zoom mechanic in Unity. Ensured all interactions felt natural and physically grounded in the environment.
05Design Approach

Designing for presence, not just usability.

VR design demands a different lens than mobile or web. The goal isn't efficiency - it's presence. Every design decision was evaluated against a single question: does this keep the user inside the world, or pull them out of it?

01
Sound before sight
In a real forest, you hear birds before you see them. The experience mirrors this - audio leads, visuals confirm. A fundamentally different interaction model from screen-based design.
02
World-space UI
Information panels appear in the environment itself, attached to birds or locations - not as overlaid HUD elements. Users lean in to read, maintaining physical engagement.
03
Reactive over scripted
Birds that respond to user movement feel alive. Small reactive behaviours - a head turn, a flutter - create the illusion of inhabiting a real ecosystem rather than a curated exhibit.
04
Voice as infrastructure
Voice guidance was designed to be ambient, not instructional. It enriches the experience rather than directing it - keeping users in a state of exploration rather than task completion.
06Learnings

What VR taught me about design.

Pankh Aur Goonj pushed me into a design context where the rules are genuinely different - no screens, no taps, no familiar affordances. The constraints were generative.

01
Sound is a design material. In screen design, audio is often an afterthought. In VR, it's the primary navigational and emotional layer. I now think of spatial audio as a core design input, not a feature.
02
Voice assistance changes access. A calm, contextual voice guide removed the need for text instructions entirely - making the experience accessible to users of any literacy level or language background.
03
Multidisciplinary collaboration requires shared language. Working across design and engineering meant constantly translating - what I called "reactive behaviour" was "event triggers" to my development teammates. Building that shared vocabulary was as important as the design work itself.
04
Small details carry outsized weight in VR. A bird turning its head to look at you takes two seconds to implement and transforms the perceived realism of the entire experience. In immersive environments, micro-interactions matter more than anywhere else.
